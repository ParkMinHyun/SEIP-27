\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Workload control.}
Our admission module builds on work that adapts per-input computation at run time under latency, resource, or thermal constraints~\cite{liu-imprecise-computation,shih-imprecise-siam,zilberstein-anytime,loop-perforation-fse,apnet-rtss,branchynet-icpr,msdnet-iclr,mcdnn-mobisys,nestdnn-mobicom,reducto-sigcomm}.
ApproxDet and Chanakya select execution configurations according to input content, resource contention, and latency--accuracy objectives~\cite{approxdet-sensys,chanakya-neurips}.
Recent mobile and embedded systems similarly adapt execution under device constraints: Agile3D selects 3D-detection branches under changing scenes and GPU contention~\cite{agile3d-mobisys}, ARIA coordinates vision inference across heterogeneous mobile processors~\cite{aria-mobisys}, and Phoenix adjusts processor placement and DNN exit depth under thermal throttling~\cite{phoenix-tecs}.
Content adaptation and brownout reduce per-request work under resource pressure~\cite{abdelzaher-content-adaptation,klein-brownout}, while Timecard uses the remaining end-to-end budget to adjust how much processing the current request receives~\cite{timecard-sosp}.
These systems adapt the amount or configuration of computation performed for the current input.
In our pipeline, the workload-control lever is optional-stage admission for the current capture.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Arrival control.}
Our pacing mechanism is related to systems that control request admission or release timing to limit queueing delay~\cite{cruz-network-calculus}.
Networking limits queue growth through active queue management and pacing~\cite{red-floyd-jacobson,codel-cacm,rfc8289,rfc8033,rfc8290,bufferbloat-cacm,tcp-pacing-infocom,bbr-queue}, while server systems admit, reject, or shed requests according to load or deadline signals~\cite{seda-sosp,dagor-socc,breakwater-osdi,aurora-load-shedding,deadline-aware-d3}.
Real-time schedulers similarly regulate job releases by stretching task periods or skipping job instances~\cite{buttazzo-elastic-task,koren-skip-over}.
Preemptive systems can instead interrupt running work to serve short or urgent requests~\cite{shinjuku-nsdi}.
Across these systems, overload can be controlled by rejecting, shedding, delaying, or preempting work.
In our pipeline, the arrival-control lever is the release timing of the next capture: an issued capture must be served, and a running stage cannot be preempted.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Coordinating workload and arrivals.}
Prior systems have combined the two control dimensions discussed above.
Feedback-control systems regulate incoming load through request admission and adjust the service level of admitted requests from deadline-miss or response-time feedback~\cite{lu-feedback-control-edf,lu-feedback-control-scheduling,welsh-overload-control}.
QoS negotiation similarly reduces service quality to accept requests that might otherwise be rejected~\cite{abdelzaher-qos-negotiation}.
These approaches establish that workload and arrival control can be coordinated.

What differs in our capture pipeline is how the two controls are coupled.
Under parallel capture, executing optional stages for the current capture determines how long the shared draft worker remains occupied, delaying subsequent captures.
Once a stage starts, the controller cannot preempt it.
The next capture's release timing determines how much of its deadline budget may be consumed waiting for that worker.
Our controller therefore coordinates the two controls across consecutive captures: stage admission decides which optional stages the current capture executes within its remaining budget, while pacing controls when the next capture is released.
To our knowledge, this is the first work to combine per-capture remaining-budget stage admission with next-capture pacing in a production camera system under a hard per-capture deadline.
